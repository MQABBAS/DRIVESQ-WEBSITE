How the ADI Part 3 is marked: 17 competencies, 51 marks, grades A and B

Rules and fees checked on GOV.UK on 25 November 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026

The ADI Part 3 has 17 areas of competence. The examiner scores each one from 0 to 3, so the most you can score is 51. Your total decides whether you fail, get a Grade B or get a Grade A. The numbers are simple once you see them laid out.

The pass marks

Total scoreResultWhat it means
0 to 30FailYou will not join the ADI register
31 to 42Grade BYou are allowed to join the ADI register
43 to 51Grade AA high standard of instruction; you are allowed to join the register

There are two ways to fail regardless of your total score: scoring 7 or less in the risk management category, or the examiner stopping the lesson because you have put yourself or someone else in danger.

What the numbers mean in practice

These are our own calculations from the official figures, useful for planning rather than official DVSA advice:

If you average…Total over 17 areasResult
1 mark per area17Fail
Just under 2 marks per area (about 1.8)31Grade B, just
2 marks per area34Grade B
2.5 marks per area (about)43Grade A, just
3 marks per area51Maximum

Put simply, you need to average a little under 2 out of 3 in every area to pass, and a little over 2.5 out of 3 for a Grade A. A single area at zero is recoverable. A weak category is the real danger, especially risk management.

The three categories

The 17 areas fall into three categories. GOV.UK lists them as lesson planning, risk management, and teaching and learning strategies, and the examiner records them on the Part 3 report form.

CategoryAreas of competenceMost you can score
Lesson planning412
Risk management515
Teaching and learning strategies824
Total1751

The counts come from the DVSA's own ADI Part 3 report form (version dated September 2022). Check the current form on GOV.UK before you rely on the detail, because DVSA can update it.

  • Lesson planning (4 areas) covers whether you worked out the pupil's goals and needs, agreed a lesson structure that suited their experience, chose suitable practice areas and adapted the plan to help them reach their goals.
  • Risk management (5 areas) carries the automatic-fail rule. It covers making sure the pupil understood how responsibility for risk would be shared, giving clear and timely directions, awareness of the surroundings and the pupil's actions, timely and appropriate intervention, and enough feedback after any safety-critical incident.
  • Teaching and learning strategies (8 areas) is the largest group. It covers a teaching style that suits the pupil, encouraging them to analyse problems and take responsibility, using examples to clarify outcomes, accurate technical information, timely feedback, following up their questions, a non-discriminatory manner, and encouraging the pupil to reflect at the end.

What each score from 0 to 3 means

On the DVSA form each competence is scored by how much of it you demonstrated:

ScoreMeaning on the form
0No evidence
1Demonstrated in a few elements
2Demonstrated in most elements
3Demonstrated in all elements

The risk management rule, with numbers

Risk management has five areas, so the most you can score there is 15. The automatic-fail rule says 7 or less fails, so you need at least 8 out of 15 in that category: an average of a little over 1.5 per area. This is our own arithmetic from the official rule. It means a lesson that is good elsewhere can still fail if one or two risk management areas score 0 or 1.

The risk management trap: a trainee can teach a lovely, thoughtful lesson and still fail because the opening conversation about responsibility was thin, directions came late, or the examiner had to step in. Check your risk management total separately in every practice lesson.

How to use this to prepare

  1. Ask whoever watches your practice lessons to score you on all 17 areas, not just give you a general impression.
  2. Add up the three categories separately. If risk management is at 7 or below out of 15, fix that before anything else.
  3. Look for the pattern across several lessons. One low score is noise. The same low score three times is a gap.
  4. Only book when your practice totals sit comfortably above 31, not on it.

Why a low risk-management score hurts so much

Examiners treat safety as the floor under everything else. A lesson can have good questioning and a clear structure, but if the examiner is not satisfied that risk was managed, the test cannot be passed. Treat the opening conversation about responsibility, and your awareness of the road and the pupil during the drive, as the first priority in your preparation.

Questions people ask

How many marks are there in the ADI Part 3?

There are 17 areas of competence, each scored 0 to 3, so the maximum is 51.

What is a pass mark on the ADI Part 3?

31 to 42 is a Grade B pass, 43 to 51 is a Grade A pass, and 0 to 30 is a fail.

Can I fail the ADI Part 3 even with a high score?

Yes. A score of 7 or less in the risk management category is an automatic fail, and so is the examiner stopping the lesson because you have put yourself or someone else in danger.

What are the three categories?

Lesson planning, risk management, and teaching and learning strategies.

Does it matter if I get a Grade A or a Grade B?

Both allow you to join the ADI register. Grade A is recorded as a high standard of instruction.
