Independent Driving — Test Section Explained

The independent driving section tests your ability to drive safely while navigating without turn-by-turn examiner directions. Understanding exactly what is assessed — and what is not — eliminates unnecessary anxiety about this test component.

What Independent Driving Involves

For approximately 20 minutes of your test, the examiner stops providing verbal directions. Instead, you navigate using one of two methods: the examiner's dashboard-mounted sat-nav (80% of tests) or road signs directing you to a named destination (20% of tests).

The critical insight: the examiner is assessing your DRIVING during this section, not your NAVIGATION. Taking a wrong turn does not generate a fault. Driving dangerously while taking that wrong turn does. This distinction removes the primary anxiety source for most candidates.

Your DriveSQ instructor incorporates independent driving practice into every lesson from approximately lesson 15 onwards, ensuring the format feels entirely familiar by test day.

Sat-Nav Navigation

The Device

The examiner uses a TomTom sat-nav mounted on the dashboard. It displays the route with a moving map, provides distance-to-turn information, and shows the road name you are heading toward. You follow this display exactly as you would your own sat-nav.

Audio Instructions

The sat-nav provides audio turn-by-turn directions. These are the same instructions you receive from any commercial sat-nav — "in 200 metres, turn left" style prompts. You may glance at the screen briefly, but extended staring is assessed as a distraction.

Route Recalculation

If you miss a turn or take a wrong direction, the sat-nav automatically recalculates. The examiner allows the recalculation to complete and you follow the new route. No fault is generated by the navigation error itself.

Road Sign Navigation

If the examiner uses road signs instead of a sat-nav, they will name a destination at the beginning of the section: "I'd like you to follow the signs to Stockport." You then navigate using road signs directing you toward that destination.

If you cannot see a sign or are unsure which direction to take, ask the examiner. They will help you — the test is not designed to catch you out with hidden or confusing signage. Your response to genuine navigational uncertainty is assessed on safety, not decisiveness.

What IS and ISN'T Assessed

Assessed

Driving quality throughout independent navigation: mirror checks, signal timing, road positioning, speed management, junction observations, hazard response. These are assessed identically to the directed portion of the test — the standard does not change.

Not Assessed

Navigation accuracy. Route choice. Speed of navigational decision-making. The direction you turn when uncertain. These navigation elements are explicitly excluded from assessment. You cannot fail for going the wrong way.

Independent Driving Tip: If you are approaching a junction and are uncertain which direction the sat-nav or signs indicate, continue straight. This is almost always the safest option and gives you time to reorientate. The sat-nav will recalculate; road signs will reappear.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is the independent driving section?
Approximately 20 minutes of the 40-minute test. During this time, you navigate independently using either road signs or the examiner's sat-nav — no verbal turn-by-turn directions from the examiner.
What happens if I go the wrong way?
Going the wrong way is NOT a fault. The examiner assesses your driving quality, not your navigation accuracy. If you take a wrong turn, the examiner redirects you back onto the planned route. Only the manner of driving is assessed.
Will I use a sat-nav or follow signs?
Approximately 80% of tests use the examiner's sat-nav. The remaining 20% require you to follow road signs to a destination named by the examiner. You must be prepared for either format.
Can I ask the examiner to repeat directions?
Yes. If you did not hear or understand a direction, ask the examiner to repeat it. This is not penalised. The examiner wants to assess your driving, not your hearing.
What if the sat-nav gives a direction I cannot safely follow?
If the sat-nav directs you to turn and you are in the wrong lane or the turn is unsafe, continue straight. The examiner recognises that safe driving takes priority over sat-nav compliance. The sat-nav will recalculate and the examiner will note your sensible decision positively.

Local Road Knowledge and Driving Education

Building local road knowledge is one of the most underestimated aspects of driver training. Knowing which junctions require extra observation, where speed limits change, and where vulnerable road users are most likely to appear turns a competent learner into a genuinely safe driver for life. DriveSQ lessons are planned around the actual roads you will drive every day after passing.

Key Driving Knowledge for Greater Manchester

  • The DVSA's recommended following distance is a 2-second gap in dry conditions — double it in rain and triple it in snow or ice.
  • Speed limits are maximums, not targets — always drive at the speed appropriate for the road conditions, visibility and traffic density.
  • Highway Code Rule 170 requires you to give way to pedestrians when turning into a side road if they are already crossing.
  • Roundabout lane selection must be decided before the give-way line — changing lanes inside a roundabout is one of the most common causes of serious test faults.

Theory test insight: The DVSA theory test bank contains 982 questions across 14 topic categories. The hazard perception section requires identifying 14 clips with one developing hazard each, plus one clip containing two developing hazards. Candidates must respond as soon as the hazard starts to develop — the optimal window is within the first 1.75 seconds of hazard development to score maximum marks.

Greater Manchester Road Network — Educational Guide

Greater Manchester's road network covers over 4,500 miles of road managed across ten metropolitan boroughs. The network includes motorways (M60, M62, M56, M61, M63), A-roads of regional importance (A6, A34, A56, A57, A580), and an extensive local road grid of B-roads and unclassified roads. Understanding the network hierarchy — and the different driving demands at each level — is a key part of driver education.

Manchester's city centre road network is managed by TfGM (Transport for Greater Manchester) using a SCOOT adaptive traffic signal system that responds to real-time vehicle detector data. This means signal phases can be shorter or longer than expected — learners should always approach signals prepared to stop rather than accelerating in anticipation of a green phase.

Motorway Rules and Joining

Motorways present some of the greatest challenges for newly qualified drivers because they combine the highest speeds with the least opportunity for error. The acceleration lane (slip road) is designed to allow a vehicle to reach motorway speed before merging — it is not a joining lane where you expect traffic to slow for you. Learners must understand that traffic on the motorway has right of way, and the responsibility to match speed and find a safe gap is entirely with the joining driver. The hard shoulder on a traditional motorway is strictly for breakdowns — stopping on the hard shoulder for any reason other than emergency or breakdown is illegal and carries a £100 fine. On smart motorways with no permanent hard shoulder, emergency refuge areas (orange-painted lay-bys) are provided every 1.5 miles. Motorway driving requires constant management of following distance — the two-second rule increases to three seconds at 70mph and should increase further in any reduced visibility or wet road condition. Fatigue is the single most significant risk on long motorway journeys — taking a 15-minute break every two hours is the minimum recommended rest schedule.

Road Markings Guide

  • Yellow line markings indicate parking restrictions — single yellow lines prohibit parking during the hours shown on the nearby sign; double yellow lines prohibit parking at any time; yellow kerb dashes restrict loading.
  • Stop lines at junctions (a solid white line across the full width of your lane) require the vehicle to stop — even if the road appears clear. Moving off before a full stop constitutes a serious fault on test and creates genuine collision risk.
  • Arrows on the road surface indicate the direction of travel required from that lane — learners must respond to road arrows as they respond to road signs, since both carry the same legal weight.
  • Speed humps, raised tables and chicanes are traffic calming measures that require a vehicle to reduce speed — driving over a speed hump at more than 15mph risks undercarriage damage and constitutes hazardous driving in a residential zone.

DVSA Practical Test — What You Need to Know

Test cancellation by the candidate requires at least three clear working days' notice to avoid forfeiture of the £62 test fee. If the DVSA cancels a test due to examiner sickness or other operational reasons, a full refund is offered. The waiting time for a test appointment varies significantly by test centre — Manchester centres typically have 4-10 week waits in normal periods, but backlogs following industrial action or pandemic disruptions can extend this to 16+ weeks.

After You Pass — What New Drivers Need to Know

Newly qualified drivers face a significant insurance premium challenge — average first-year premiums for 17-21 year olds exceed £2,000 annually. Several evidence-based strategies reduce this cost: adding an experienced named driver (not as a fronting arrangement — the young driver must be the primary user), selecting a lower-insurance group vehicle, increasing the voluntary excess, and installing a telematics device. Telematics policies monitor acceleration, braking, cornering, speed and time of driving — young drivers who score well typically see 20-40% premium reductions at renewal. Pass Plus qualification reduces premiums with many major insurers by up to 35%.
