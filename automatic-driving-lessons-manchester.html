Why Choose Automatic?

The Real Advantages of Learning Automatic

Automatic cars remove the biggest technical barriers to learning — here's what that actually means in practice.

Stalling Is Impossible
In a manual, stalling at junctions, roundabouts, and traffic lights is one of the most common causes of test failure — and one of the biggest sources of learner anxiety. An automatic car physically cannot stall. That's not a small advantage.
More Mental Space for the Road
In a manual, a beginner's cognitive load is split between clutch control, gear selection, observations, steering, and mirrors. Automatic removes two of those demands entirely — freeing up more attention for hazard awareness, which is what the DVSA actually tests.
Faster Progress for Many Learners
Without clutch control and gear-change drills to master, many learners reach test-ready standard in fewer hours. The DVSA average is 45 hours of professional tuition — many automatic DriveSQ learners hit this in 30–40 hours.
Perfect for City Driving
Manchester city centre — Oxford Road, Deansgate, Piccadilly — is stop-start traffic at its most relentless. An automatic car handles constant gear changes silently in the background. No slipping the clutch. No biting point anxiety at every set of lights.
All Electric Cars Are Automatic
Every electric vehicle — from a Tesla to a Nissan Leaf — is automatic by design. As the UK moves toward an EV future, learning automatic positions you for the technology of the next 20 years. A manual licence won't help you with a Kia EV9.
Significantly Less Stressful
Many learners who struggle with manual lessons — especially those with anxiety, older learners returning to driving, or those with physical limitations — find automatic lessons transformative. The car cooperates with you rather than adding another set of demands to manage.

The Truth About Automatic Licences

What nobody tells you — from a school that teaches both.

⚠️ What an Automatic Licence Actually Means

Passing your test in an automatic car means your DVSA licence carries an automatic-only restriction (code 78). You cannot legally drive a manual (gear-change) car without taking a separate manual practical test — there is no exemption and no upgrade process. You start the manual test from scratch.

This is the one genuine downside of automatic — and it's worth knowing upfront. If you ever plan to drive a manual vehicle (a van for work, a hire car abroad, a friend's car in an emergency), you would need to either book further manual lessons and a test, or always ensure you have access to an automatic.

However: for the majority of people, this restriction is perfectly manageable. Most modern cars sold in the UK — and virtually all new electric vehicles — are automatic. If you will use your licence primarily for a car you own or lease, an automatic licence is rarely limiting in practice.

🚫 Debunking "Automatic Is Cheating"

This myth — common among older drivers who learned in an era when automatics were rare — is simply outdated. The DVSA practical test has identical pass criteria whether you're in an automatic or a manual. Examiners assess exactly the same skills: observation, hazard awareness, speed management, positioning, manoeuvres, and independent driving.

In some respects, automatic test candidates face a harder examination of pure road craft — because there are no clutch-related faults as a buffer. Examiners focus entirely on how you read the road.

A driving licence is a driving licence. It says "Category B" on the front regardless of transmission type. No employer, no insurance company, and no motorway junction cares whether you passed in a Vauxhall Astra manual or a Toyota Yaris automatic. The only practical restriction is the one you know about: you're limited to automatics.

The "cheating" narrative belongs in the 1980s. Leave it there.

Can I drive a manual car after passing in an automatic? +

No. Passing your practical test in an automatic car means your DVSA licence carries a restriction (code 78) to automatic vehicles only. You cannot legally drive a manual car without completing a separate manual practical test. However, this doesn't require taking the theory test again — your theory certificate remains valid. Many people are perfectly happy with an automatic-only licence, and all electric vehicles are automatic by design, so the restriction becomes less relevant as the UK transitions to EVs.

How many automatic driving lessons do I need in Manchester? +

The DVSA average across all learners is 45 hours of professional instruction. In practice, automatic learners often reach test-ready standard in 30–40 hours, because the absence of clutch control and gear-change drills removes a large chunk of the learning curve. Nervous learners, older learners starting from scratch, or those who have previously struggled with manual lessons typically need 40–50 hours. We'll give you an honest, personalised assessment after your first lesson.

Are automatic driving tests harder? +

No. The DVSA practical test syllabus is identical regardless of whether you sit in an automatic or a manual car. Examiners mark the same competencies: observation, hazard awareness, speed control, positioning, manoeuvres, and the independent driving section. If anything, there are fewer possible failure points in an automatic — stalling, poor clutch control, and wrong gear selection simply cannot occur. You won't be penalised for being in an automatic car.

Can I do Pass Plus in an automatic? +

Yes, absolutely. Pass Plus is available to all newly qualified drivers regardless of whether they passed in an automatic or a manual car. It covers motorway driving, night driving, rural road driving, dual carriageways, and adverse weather. DriveSQ offers Pass Plus in Greater Manchester in our automatic vehicles. It can reduce your car insurance premiums with some providers — WhatsApp us to enquire.

Does automatic suit nervous drivers? +

Absolutely — and it's one of the most common reasons people switch to DriveSQ automatic lessons. Stalling, which causes significant anxiety for many nervous learners at roundabouts, junctions, and traffic lights, is physically impossible in an automatic car. Without the clutch and gear lever competing for mental attention, anxious learners can focus entirely on the road, their positioning, and other traffic. Many of our most nervous learners have gone on to pass first time in an automatic after struggling significantly with manual lessons at other schools. If anxiety is a factor in your learning, automatic is worth serious consideration.
