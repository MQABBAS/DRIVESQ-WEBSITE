Serious Faults — What Actually Fails Your Test

Understanding the precise boundary between a driving fault and a serious fault empowers you to focus your test preparation on the errors that actually matter. This guide explains every serious fault category with real Manchester test examples.

Fault Categories

The DVSA driving test assessment uses three fault levels: driving faults (minor errors that do not compromise safety), serious faults (errors creating potential danger), and dangerous faults (errors creating actual danger requiring examiner intervention). One serious or dangerous fault fails the test.

Driving Fault

A less-than-perfect action that does not create danger. Examples: slightly wide positioning on a turn, checking mirrors a moment late, signalling fractionally early. You can accumulate up to 15 without failing. These are normal imperfections — even experienced drivers make them daily.

Serious Fault

An error creating potential danger. Examples: emerging from a junction without adequate observation, incorrect lane positioning forcing other traffic to take evasive action, failure to respond to a red traffic light. One serious fault fails the test regardless of other performance.

Dangerous Fault

An error requiring the examiner to take physical or verbal action to prevent actual danger. Examples: pulling into oncoming traffic, running a red light at speed, failing to stop for a pedestrian on a crossing. Examiner intervention via dual controls or verbal command results in dangerous fault marking.

Top 10 Serious Faults on Manchester Tests

1. Junction Observations

Pulling out from T-junctions, roundabouts, or crossroads without adequate checks. The fix: establish a consistent observation routine — stop, look right, left, right again, assess, proceed only with genuine clearance.

2. Steering Control

Veering across lane markings, mounting kerbs during manoeuvres, or crossing the centre line on bends. Caused by looking at the steering wheel instead of where you want to go — eyes lead, hands follow.

3. Mirror Neglect

Failing to check mirrors before signalling, changing speed, or altering course. The mirror-signal-manoeuvre sequence must be unbreakable. Practise until mirror checks are automatic, not conscious decisions.

4. Positioning

Wrong lane on roundabouts, straddling lane markings on dual carriageways, or approaching right turns from the left side of the road. Route familiarity around your test centre eliminates most positioning errors.

5. Reverse Manoeuvres

Poor observation during reversing — failing to check all around before and during the manoeuvre. The manoeuvre itself can be slightly imperfect; the observation must be consistently thorough.

6. Speed Management

Approaching hazards too fast, exceeding speed limits, or driving too slowly without justification. Maintain awareness of posted limits and adjust speed to match road conditions.

7. Response to Signals

Proceeding on red lights, ignoring stop signs, or failing to respond to road markings. Genuine observation plus knowledge of signal meanings prevents these avoidable faults.

8. Moving Off Unsafely

Pulling away from the kerb without checking mirrors and blind spots. Every moving-off sequence must include: mirror check, signal if necessary, blind spot check, move when safe.

Prevention Strategy: 80% of serious faults stem from observation failures, not skill failures. Your vehicle control may be excellent, but one missed mirror check at the wrong moment causes test failure. DriveSQ's final preparation emphasises observation discipline above all other skills.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a serious fault?
A serious fault (previously called a major) is an error that creates actual or potential danger to yourself, the examiner, other road users, or property. A single serious fault results in test failure regardless of how well you performed otherwise.
How many driving faults can I get and still pass?
You can accumulate up to 15 driving faults (minors) and still pass. However, if you receive 16 or more driving faults, or if you accumulate 3 or more driving faults in the same category, the examiner may mark it as a serious fault.
What is the most common serious fault?
Junction observations — failing to check adequately before emerging from junctions — is consistently the most recorded serious fault nationally and across all Manchester test centres. It accounts for approximately 20% of all serious fault markings.
Can the examiner fail me for going too slowly?
Yes. Driving significantly below the speed limit without justification can be marked as a serious fault under "progress" if it causes potential danger to other road users (forcing following vehicles to brake or overtake dangerously).
If I make one mistake, should I give up?
Absolutely not. A single driving fault is not a failure. Even several driving faults still result in a pass. Do not dwell on mistakes — reset your focus and continue driving to your standard. Many candidates pass with 8-12 driving faults.

Local Road Knowledge and Driving Education

Building local road knowledge is one of the most underestimated aspects of driver training. Knowing which junctions require extra observation, where speed limits change, and where vulnerable road users are most likely to appear turns a competent learner into a genuinely safe driver for life. DriveSQ lessons are planned around the actual roads you will drive every day after passing.

Key Driving Knowledge for Greater Manchester

  • Mirror checks should happen every 5-8 seconds on open roads and before every speed change, direction change or manoeuvre.
  • The DVSA's recommended following distance is a 2-second gap in dry conditions — double it in rain and triple it in snow or ice.
  • Speed limits are maximums, not targets — always drive at the speed appropriate for the road conditions, visibility and traffic density.
  • Highway Code Rule 170 requires you to give way to pedestrians when turning into a side road if they are already crossing.

Stopping distances: The Highway Code stopping distances — based on a 13.5-metre car in dry conditions — are the minimum legal reference points. In wet conditions, stopping distances at least double. In icy conditions, stopping distances can be ten times greater than dry distances. At 70mph on ice, a car travelling with 13.5 metres of following distance needs 750 metres to stop safely — over half a mile. These figures make a compelling case for reduced speed in adverse conditions.

Greater Manchester Road Network — Educational Guide

Greater Manchester's road network covers over 4,500 miles of road managed across ten metropolitan boroughs. The network includes motorways (M60, M62, M56, M61, M63), A-roads of regional importance (A6, A34, A56, A57, A580), and an extensive local road grid of B-roads and unclassified roads. Manchester's city centre road network is managed by TfGM (Transport for Greater Manchester) using a SCOOT adaptive traffic signal system that responds to real-time vehicle detector data. This means signal phases can be shorter or longer than expected — learners should always approach signals prepared to stop rather than accelerating in anticipation of a green phase.

Vehicle Safety Checks

Pre-drive vehicle safety checks are not just a driving test requirement — they are a legal responsibility for every driver every time they drive. The DVSA's recommended pre-drive checks can be remembered as POWDERS: Petrol (or charge level for EVs), Oil, Water (coolant and windscreen wash), Damage (visible exterior damage that might affect road safety), Electrics (lights, indicators, horn), Rubber (tyres and wiper blades), and Seatbelts (and mirrors). Oil level should be checked on a level surface with the engine cold — the dipstick should show oil between the minimum and maximum marks; below minimum requires immediate topping up before driving. Tyre pressure should be checked monthly and before long journeys using a calibrated gauge — the correct pressure for your vehicle is found in the owner's manual, on the door jamb sticker, or inside the fuel filler cap. Windscreen wash concentration should be set to the appropriate dilution for the season — in winter, a 50/50 antifreeze-to-water mix prevents washer fluid from freezing in the nozzles at temperatures below -10°C. Working headlights, brake lights and indicators are legal requirements — checking them regularly using a wall reflection at night or asking a helper to confirm operation keeps you legal and safe.

Lane Discipline Essentials

  • Hatched areas (diagonal white lines inside a solid white border) must not be entered except in an emergency — hatching inside a broken white border may be entered if safe and necessary.
  • Bus lanes that operate during off-peak hours may be used by general traffic outside their hours of operation — always check the hours-of-operation sign before entering a bus lane.
  • The left lane on any multi-lane road (including dual carriageways) is the correct lane for normal driving — the centre and right lanes are overtaking lanes only.
  • If you are in the right lane of a three-lane motorway and a vehicle behind signals to overtake, you should return to the middle lane and then the left lane as soon as it is safe to do so.

DVSA Practical Test — What You Need to Know

The DVSA practical driving test has a duration of approximately 40-50 minutes for most candidates. Within this time the examiner will assess: a 'show me' safety question while driving, approximately 20 minutes of independent driving following a sat-nav or road signs, at least one manoeuvre, and the possibility (one in three tests) of an emergency stop exercise. The examiner may ask you to pull over and restart on several occasions — this is normal and does not indicate impending failure.

After You Pass — What New Drivers Need to Know

Vehicle maintenance responsibility transfers entirely to the driver after passing — and many new drivers are unaware of what this involves. Key monthly checks: tyre pressure (including spare), tyre tread depth (legal minimum 1.6mm across the central 75% of the tread width and around the entire circumference), oil level, coolant level, windscreen washer fluid, and all lights. Annual MOT from year three, six-monthly visual tyre inspection, and a full service to manufacturer's schedule. Driving a vehicle with a tyre tread below 1.6mm carries a £2,500 fine and three penalty points per tyre — four defective tyres could mean 12 points and licence revocation for a newly qualified driver.
